# HELOCs: A Practical Guide

A home equity line of credit (HELOC) lets a homeowner borrow against the equity they've built in their home.

Equity is the difference between what your home is currently worth and what you still owe on it. A HELOC turns part of that equity into a line of credit you can draw from as needed.

It works more like a credit card than a traditional loan: you're approved for a limit, you draw what you need when you need it, and you only pay interest on the amount you've actually borrowed.

### Relationship to Your Existing Mortgage

A HELOC is a separate loan, not an addition to your mortgage. Your first mortgage stays exactly as it is, with the same rate, payment, and servicer. The HELOC sits behind it as a "second lien," which is why it's often called a second mortgage.

This matters most when you have a low-rate first mortgage. A HELOC lets you access equity without refinancing the entire mortgage at a higher rate.

### What "Secured by the House" Means

A secured loan is backed by collateral. The lender records a lien against the property, giving them a legal claim to it if you stop paying. In a default, they can foreclose, force a sale, and recover what they're owed from the proceeds.

Because the lender has a valuable asset backing the debt, their risk is much lower than with an unsecured loan, and they pass that on as a lower interest rate.

HELOC rates are still usually higher than first mortgage rates because the HELOC is second in line. In a foreclosure, the first mortgage is paid off completely before the HELOC lender recovers anything.

## How It Works

### Draw Period

Typically around 10 years. You can borrow, repay, and borrow again up to your limit. Minimum payments are often interest-only during this phase.

### Repayment Period

Typically 10 to 20 years. You can no longer draw from the line, and payments increase because you're now paying down principal as well as interest.

### Borrowing Limits

Lenders cap combined loan-to-value (CLTV), meaning your first mortgage plus the HELOC, at roughly 80 to 85% of the home's appraised value.

### Rates

Rates are usually variable, tied to the prime rate plus a margin set by the lender. For example, a prime rate of 6.75% plus a 0.75% margin gives a 7.50% HELOC rate. As of mid-2026, the national average sat around 7.2% for borrowers with strong credit and moderate CLTV. Some lenders allow you to lock part of your balance at a fixed rate.

### Fees

Closing costs are often modest or waived, but look for annual fees, early closure fees, minimum draw requirements, and appraisal costs.

## Worked Example

| Item | Amount |
| -------------------------- | ---------- |
| Appraised home value | $1,000,000 |
| Remaining mortgage balance | $600,000 |
| Current equity | $400,000 |
| Max total debt at 80% CLTV | $800,000 |
| Max HELOC at 80% CLTV | $200,000 |
| Max HELOC at 85% CLTV | $250,000 |

Borrowing $75,000 to $125,000 in this scenario puts CLTV around 68 to 73%, a comfortable range where lenders tend to offer better pricing.

Note that equity is based on the current appraised value, not simply on how much principal has been paid. Appreciation increases equity, and a price drop reduces it.

## What People Use HELOCs For

- Home improvements: kitchen and bath remodels, additions, finished basements or garages, ADUs, roofs, HVAC, solar, windows, and major repairs like foundations or sewer lines. Generally considered the strongest use case.
- Debt consolidation: replacing high-interest credit card or personal loan balances with a lower rate. Can save a lot, but converts unsecured debt into debt backed by the house.
- Emergency backstop: an open line kept at a zero balance as a reserve for job loss or large surprise expenses.
- Bridge financing: using equity in a current home toward a down payment on the next one before the first sells.
- Education: tuition, sometimes as an alternative to private student loans. Federal student loans usually offer better protections.
- Other large expenses: medical bills, business funding, weddings, or vehicles. These call for more caution since they don't build value in the home.

## Why Renovations Are the Classic Use Case

Renovations keep the borrowed money at least partially tied to the asset securing the loan. However, they are not a pure investment. Most projects recoup only a portion of their cost at resale, often in the 50 to 80% range.

- Quality of life: the main benefit. If you stay for years, the value of living in the improved home is real, and partial recoup at sale lowers the true net cost.
- Maintenance protects value: roofs, HVAC, and plumbing rarely raise the sale price much, but neglecting them hurts it badly during inspections and negotiations.
- Some projects are true investments: ADUs in California can add substantial value and generate rental income. Added bedrooms or square footage can come close to or exceed cost in some markets.
- Tax benefits: HELOC interest may be deductible when funds are used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home (only if you itemize). Capital improvements also add to your cost basis, reducing taxable gain when you sell.

## When to Reach for a HELOC

- Costs are uncertain or spread over time, such as renovations paid in contractor milestones.
- You have a low-rate first mortgage you don't want to refinance.
- The amount is large (six figures), where personal loans are often capped or expensive.
- You have stable income and can comfortably handle higher payments if rates rise.
- You want flexible access to funds without paying interest on money you haven't used.
- You plan to stay in the home long enough to benefit from the improvements.

## When to Consider Alternatives

- Home equity loan (HEL): same collateral, but a lump sum at a fixed rate with fixed payments. Better when you know the exact amount upfront and want predictability.
- Personal loan: better for smaller amounts (under roughly $20k to $30k) or when you don't want the house on the line. Higher rates, but faster funding and no risk to the home.
- Cash-out refinance: only worth considering if your current mortgage rate is at or above today's rates.
- Cash savings: if you can pay for a project without borrowing, you avoid interest and risk entirely.

## Things to Watch Out For

- Your home is collateral: failing to repay can lead to foreclosure. This is the most important risk to keep in mind.
- Variable rates: payments rise when the prime rate rises. Model your budget at rates a few points higher than today.
- Payment shock: moving from interest-only draw period payments to full principal and interest can increase payments significantly.
- Line freezes: lenders can freeze or reduce your line if home values drop or your finances change, which happened widely in 2008.
- Overborrowing: easy access to a large line makes it tempting to spend on things that don't add value.
- Debt consolidation traps: paying off cards with a HELOC only works if spending habits change; otherwise you end up with both.
- Fees and fine print: annual fees, early closure penalties, minimum draws, and conditions on fixed-rate conversions.
- Selling the home: the HELOC must be paid off at sale, reducing your proceeds.
- Falling home values: borrowing near the CLTV limit leaves little cushion if prices decline.
- Credit impact: a new line adds a hard inquiry, and a heavily used line can affect your credit profile.

## Shopping Checklist

- Get quotes from at least three lenders, including a credit union.
- Compare the margin over prime, not just the introductory rate.
- Ask about draw and repayment period lengths and minimum payment structure.
- Ask about fixed-rate conversion options and their costs.
- Confirm all fees: closing, annual, early closure, and appraisal.
- Check the rate cap: how high the variable rate can go over the life of the line.
- Ask how long approval and funding take, since appraisals can add weeks.
- Ask whether you can pay down principal early during the draw period without penalty.
- Keep receipts for all capital improvements for tax purposes.

## Bottom Line

A HELOC is a flexible, relatively low-cost way to fund large expenses, especially renovations, when you have meaningful equity and a mortgage rate you want to keep. The trade-off is that your home secures the debt, so it works best with stable income, a clear plan for the money, and a budget that can absorb higher rates.
