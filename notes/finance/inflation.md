# Inflation

Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services. When prices rise, each unit of currency buys less, so purchasing power falls. Inflation does not mean that every price rises at the same time or by the same amount; it describes the average change across a broad basket of goods and services.

## Measuring inflation

Inflation is usually reported as the percentage change in a price index over a stated period. Common U.S. measures include:

- **Consumer Price Index (CPI):** Tracks prices paid by households for a representative basket of goods and services.
- **Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index:** Tracks prices of goods and services consumed by households and accounts more readily for changes in what consumers buy. It is the inflation measure associated with the Federal Reserve's 2% longer-run goal.
- **Headline inflation:** Includes all items in the index, including volatile food and energy prices.
- **Core inflation:** Excludes food and energy to make underlying trends easier to assess; it is not a measure of the actual cost of living because households still buy food and energy.

The inflation rate for an individual household can differ substantially from the published average because spending patterns vary. A household that spends heavily on rent, gasoline, or medical care may experience a different inflation rate from the headline index.

A modest, stable inflation rate is common in a growing economy. The problem is not simply that prices are higher than they were in the past; it is that prices are rising rapidly, unpredictably, or faster than incomes and returns on savings.

## Why inflation happens

Inflation usually reflects several forces at once. The most useful distinction is between the initial source of price pressure and the factors that allow it to persist.

| Driver | What happens | Important qualification |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Demand-driven inflation | Household, business, or government demand exceeds the economy's near-term ability to produce goods and services. | Demand can be supported by strong income growth, fiscal stimulus, easy credit, or accumulated savings. |
| Cost-push inflation | Input costs such as energy, materials, transportation, or labor rise, and businesses pass some of the increase through to prices. | A cost increase may raise the price of one category without causing broad, persistent inflation. |
| Supply shocks | Events such as pandemics, wars, crop failures, or natural disasters reduce the availability of important goods or services. | The initial shock is often temporary; it becomes more persistent when expectations, wages, or demand keep prices moving higher. |
| Money and credit growth | Money and credit expand rapidly relative to the economy's ability to produce goods and services, adding demand pressure. | Money growth alone does not mechanically cause inflation; its effect depends on velocity, output, expectations, and financial conditions. |
| Expectations and persistence | Households and businesses begin to expect continuing inflation, so workers seek higher pay and firms adjust prices more frequently. | Wage growth and price increases are not automatically a self-reinforcing spiral; the outcome depends on productivity, demand, and expectations. |

## Effects of inflation

- **Reduced purchasing power:** A paycheck or cash balance buys fewer goods and services over time.
- **Uneven household impact:** Lower-income households often feel inflation more acutely because necessities consume a larger share of their budgets. People with fixed incomes can also be exposed unless their benefits or income adjust.
- **Redistribution between borrowers and lenders:** Unexpected inflation generally reduces the real burden of fixed-rate debt and reduces the real value of fixed payments received by lenders. Variable-rate debt can become more expensive as interest rates adjust.
- **Lower real returns:** The return on a savings account, bond, or investment should be compared with inflation and taxes, not considered in nominal terms alone.
- **More uncertainty:** Unpredictable prices make it harder for households to budget and for businesses to plan, invest, hire, and set prices.
- **Financial-market repricing:** Inflation can push interest rates higher, lowering the market value of existing fixed-rate bonds. It can also affect stock valuations, exchange rates, and real-estate prices, but no asset is guaranteed to rise with inflation.

For a rough illustration, if savings earn 1% while inflation is 5%, the exact pre-tax real return is:

`(1.01 / 1.05) - 1 = -3.81%`

The commonly used approximation is `1% - 5% = -4%`. Both figures describe a meaningful loss of purchasing power before taxes and fees.

## How the Federal Reserve and government respond

There is no single policy that removes inflation without tradeoffs. The appropriate response depends on whether inflation is being driven mainly by demand, supply, expectations, or some combination.

| Tool | How it works | Limits and tradeoffs |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Higher policy rates | The Federal Reserve raises the federal funds target range, increasing borrowing costs and generally reducing interest-sensitive demand. | Policy works with uncertain lags and can slow hiring, investment, and housing activity. It cannot directly produce more oil, housing, or semiconductors. |
| Quantitative tightening (QT) | The Fed allows securities to mature without fully reinvesting the proceeds and can also sell securities in some circumstances, reducing the size of its balance sheet. | QT can tighten financial conditions, but it is not simply a process of "pulling money out" of the economy; its effects depend on reserves, market conditions, and expectations. |
| Forward guidance | The Fed communicates its reaction function, policy outlook, and inflation goal to influence expectations and financial conditions. | Guidance is effective only to the extent that households and markets find it credible. |
| Fiscal restraint | The government can reduce demand through lower spending or higher taxes, or end temporary stimulus programs. | Fiscal changes can be slow to enact and may conflict with employment or distributional goals. |
| Supply-side measures | Measures such as removing bottlenecks, reducing unnecessary trade barriers, or temporarily releasing oil reserves can improve supply or reduce specific price pressures. | These measures may help individual categories or short-term disruptions but usually cannot substitute for demand management when inflation is broad and persistent. |

The Fed is generally balancing its dual mandate of maximum employment and stable prices. Higher rates can reduce inflation by weakening demand, but they do not fix every supply problem. Policy decisions therefore involve a tradeoff between bringing inflation down and avoiding unnecessary damage to employment and output.

## If high inflation persists

- **Tighter policy and slower growth:** Persistent inflation can require higher rates for longer, reducing borrowing, housing activity, business investment, and consumer demand.
- **Recession risk:** A sharp slowdown can lead to layoffs and falling output, but high inflation does not guarantee a recession.
- **Stagflation:** Inflation can remain high while growth weakens and unemployment rises. This is especially difficult because policies that reduce demand can worsen the employment side of the problem.
- **Exchange-rate pressure:** Persistent inflation can weaken a currency if it reduces confidence relative to other countries, making imports more expensive. Exchange rates also depend on interest rates, fiscal conditions, trade, and global demand, so depreciation is not automatic.
- **Hyperinflation:** This is an extreme and rare breakdown in the currency's usefulness, usually associated with severe fiscal, monetary, and institutional failures. Historical examples include Weimar Germany, Zimbabwe, and Venezuela.

## What households can do

- **Track your own spending:** Use your actual budget rather than the headline inflation rate to identify the categories affecting you most. Compare prices, substitute products where practical, and reduce discretionary spending if necessary.
- **Keep an emergency fund liquid:** A high-yield savings account or short-term Treasury instrument may earn more than a basic checking account, but its yield is not guaranteed to beat inflation. Liquidity and safety matter more than maximizing return for emergency cash.
- **Reduce expensive variable-rate debt:** Paying down credit-card balances and other high-rate debt can provide a more reliable benefit than trying to earn a return elsewhere. Refinancing variable-rate debt into a fixed rate can reduce payment uncertainty when the terms are favorable.
- **Understand fixed-rate borrowing:** A fixed-rate loan protects the payment from future rate increases, but locking in a rate is not automatically beneficial; compare the rate, fees, expected time in the loan, and the possibility of refinancing later.
- **Use inflation-linked assets deliberately:** TIPS adjust principal with changes in the CPI, while Series I savings bonds have a composite rate that includes an inflation component. Both have specific terms, limits, liquidity rules, and tax treatment.
- **Match investments to the time horizon:** Diversified stocks and real estate may help preserve purchasing power over long periods, but they can fall sharply and are not reliable short-term inflation hedges. Do not take more risk merely because inflation is high.
- **Review income:** Keep compensation, benefits, and savings goals under review. A raise that does not keep pace with your personal cost increases may still be an improvement, but the real change in purchasing power is what matters.

## Key takeaway

Inflation is a broad change in purchasing power, not just a rise in one price. Demand, supply, money and credit, and expectations can all contribute, and the policy response depends on which forces are dominant. For households, the durable defenses are maintaining liquidity, limiting expensive debt, evaluating returns after inflation and taxes, and matching investment risk to the time horizon.
