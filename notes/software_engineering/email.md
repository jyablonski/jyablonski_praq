# Email Deliverability: Bounces, Suppression, and Ownership

Email gives companies a direct relationship with their audience, but maintaining that channel requires actively managing unreachable addresses and delivery failures.

The main email categories are:

- **Editorial/newsletter:** Recurring content sent on a schedule
- **Marketing:** Promotional campaigns subject to consent and unsubscribe rules
- **Transactional:** Receipts, password resets, and other messages triggered by user actions

This distinction matters because marketing opt-outs should not normally block essential transactional messages.

## ESP and Sender Responsibilities

An Email Service Provider (ESP) handles the delivery infrastructure:

- Sending IPs and domains
- SPF, DKIM, and DMARC authentication
- Provider reputation and throttling
- Retries and connection handling
- Delivery, bounce, and engagement events
- Lists, segments, templates, and campaign scheduling

The ESP records what receiving mail servers report, but the sender generally decides when an address should stop receiving mail.

| Responsibility | Owner |
| -------------------------------------- | ------ |
| Classify SMTP responses and bounces | ESP |
| Retry temporary failures within a send | ESP |
| Record delivery events | ESP |
| Define when to stop sending | Sender |
| Enforce and audit suppression | Sender |

Bounce state is usually read-only because it represents delivery telemetry generated by the ESP. Senders cannot relabel an address as hard-bounced, but they can suppress it. Relabeling changes the recorded event; suppression controls future behavior.

## Why Addresses Become Unreachable

- **Consent withdrawal:** The recipient unsubscribes or reports spam.
- **Invalid or abandoned address:** The address was mistyped, an employee left, or a mailbox or domain was retired.
- **Mailbox neglect:** The mailbox exists but remains full or unmonitored.
- **Sender reputation:** The mailbox is valid, but its provider rejects or throttles the sender.

Consent changes are explicit. The other cases usually surface through delivery failures and require interpreting the SMTP response, not just the bounce label.

## Hard and Soft Bounces

### Hard Bounces

A hard bounce is a permanent failure, usually represented by an SMTP `5xx` response. Common causes include a nonexistent address, an invalid domain, or a permanent policy rejection.

Not every hard bounce means the address is invalid. Reputation blocks may also return `5xx` responses for valid mailboxes.

Use the distribution of failures to distinguish the likely cause:

- Failures spread across many receiving domains usually indicate poor list quality.
- Failures concentrated at one provider usually indicate a sender reputation or configuration problem.

Suppressing every address during a provider-specific block discards valid subscribers without fixing the underlying issue.

### Soft Bounces

A soft bounce is a temporary failure, usually represented by an SMTP `4xx` response. Common causes include:

- Full mailbox
- Provider outage or overload
- Greylisting or rate limiting
- Connection timeout
- Message size limits

The ESP normally retries these failures with backoff before reporting the final outcome of the send.

### Repeated Soft Bounces

Do not assume repeated soft bounces eventually become hard bounces. Some ESPs promote an address after several failed sends, while others continue attempting delivery indefinitely.

> Confirm how the ESP handles repeated soft bounces, including its thresholds and whether they are configurable.

Unchecked soft bounces can cause:

- **Reputation damage:** Repeatedly sending into provider throttling shows poor handling of delivery feedback.
- **Spam-trap exposure:** Providers may eventually recycle abandoned addresses into spam traps.
- **Engagement dilution:** Dead profiles depress engagement rates and weaken audience targeting.

## Building a Suppression Policy

### 1. Capture Delivery Events

Store delivery events in the warehouse through webhooks, bulk exports, or both. At minimum, retain:

- Recipient
- Message ID and send timestamp
- Delivery status and bounce type
- SMTP code and reason
- Receiving domain

The reason is essential: a full mailbox and greylisting may both be soft bounces but should not lead to the same decision.

### 2. Model Address State

Track consecutive failures across distinct sends. Reset the streak after a successful delivery or a reliable engagement event such as a click. Keep this layer factual so policy can change without rebuilding the underlying history.

### 3. Define Auditable Rules

Keep thresholds in version-controlled configuration rather than embedding them in query logic:

```yaml
suppression_consecutive_soft_bounces: 5
suppression_no_engagement_days: 30
domain_soft_bounce_spike_multiplier: 3.0
```

A reasonable starting rule is to suppress after a threshold of consecutive soft bounces and no recent engagement. Tune the values using actual delivery data.

Bucket bounce reasons by whether they should count toward suppression. For example, sustained `mailbox full` responses may indicate an abandoned mailbox, while greylisting and connection timeouts usually should not accumulate.

### 4. Guard Against Provider-Wide Incidents

Provider throttling can generate a large number of soft bounces for valid addresses. A naive streak counter may then suppress an entire provider's audience.

Compare each receiving domain's daily soft-bounce rate with its trailing baseline. Freeze suppression for domains experiencing an unusual spike, and only evaluate their addresses after delivery returns to normal.

### 5. Produce Current State

Maintain the suppression flag in the warehouse as the source of truth, including the reason and timestamp. Sync current state rather than only appending suppression events so an address can become eligible again after a temporary delivery incident.

## Enforcement

Suppression is usually enforced in one of two ways:

- **Platform suppression:** A global blocklist enforced by the ESP across every send
- **Audience exclusion:** A maintained segment that campaigns and automations must exclude

Platform enforcement is stronger because it cannot be accidentally omitted. If only audience exclusions are available, build suppression into standard target-audience definitions rather than relying on a checkbox for every campaign.

Audit every send path:

- One-off campaigns
- Automated and lifecycle flows
- Transactional API sends, which may bypass audience rules

Apply separate rules where transactional delivery must continue despite a marketing suppression.

## Rollout and Monitoring

Before enabling suppression:

1. Break candidates down by receiving domain and bounce reason.
1. Investigate candidates clustered at one provider as a possible reputation incident.
1. Sanity-check the population size; an unexpectedly large share may indicate bad acquisition data or an unrecognized provider block.
1. Start with the highest-confidence cases, such as long streaks with consistent dead-mailbox reasons.
1. Expand after monitoring delivery and bounce metrics through at least one send cycle.

Continuously monitor:

- Addresses with long failure streaks
- New suppressions and reversals
- Soft-bounce rate by receiving domain
- Sudden changes in bounce reasons

Alert on unusual growth rather than relying on occasional manual audits.

## Prevention

Suppression handles existing problems. Reduce new ones with:

- Address validation at signup
- Confirmed opt-in
- Acquisition-source tracking
- Periodic list verification
- Engagement-based sunsetting for long-inactive recipients

## Key Takeaways

1. SMTP response type alone does not reveal whether the address or the sender is the problem.
1. Verify whether the ESP promotes repeated soft bounces; some do not.
1. The ESP owns delivery telemetry, while the sender owns suppression policy.
1. Base suppression on consecutive failures, bounce reasons, and recent engagement.
1. Pause suppression during provider-wide bounce spikes to avoid removing valid subscribers.
1. Enforce the policy across every marketing, automation, and transactional send path.
1. Keep suppression state and its rationale in the warehouse for auditing and recovery.
