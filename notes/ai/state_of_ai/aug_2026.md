# The State of AI — August 2026

The most useful way to think about the current AI boom is to separate three questions that are often collapsed into one.\\

- Is the technology real?
- Will the companies building and selling it earn attractive returns?
- And will the transition be socially manageable?

The answer to the first can be yes even if the answers to the other two are no.

AI systems are already useful enough to change how software, research, customer support, media, and administrative work are performed. That does not mean every model company will survive, every data center will earn its cost of capital, or the benefits will arrive quickly enough and be distributed broadly enough to offset the disruption.

- Railways and fiber networks created enormous long-term value while destroying large amounts of investor capital along the way.

AI may follow the same pattern: transformative infrastructure, poor returns for many of its financiers, and a difficult adjustment for everyone whose livelihood is exposed to it.

## A real technology inside a speculative buildout

The case for the current infrastructure boom is not irrational. The major cloud providers say they remain constrained by available compute and, increasingly, by electricity and grid interconnections. Their AI-exposed businesses are growing, and some demand cannot be served because capacity is not ready. If AI proves as important as its strongest results suggest, being compute-short could cost a platform years of market position. From the perspective of any one hyperscaler, overbuilding is a survivable mistake; allowing a competitor to establish an unassailable lead may not be.

That logic becomes less convincing when applied to the industry as a whole. Every participant can make a rational decision to build while their combined decisions produce far more capacity than can earn an adequate return. Capital expenditure has diverged sharply from directly attributable revenue, and much of the spending is going into accelerators whose economic life may be shorter than their accounting life. New chips and inference-specific hardware do not merely add capacity; they can reduce the value of the hardware already installed. If useful life estimates eventually have to be shortened, depreciation and reported earnings could be hit across the sector at the same time.

This is why arguments over whether AI is a “bubble” are often unproductive. A bubble does not require the underlying technology to be fake. It only requires asset prices, financing assumptions, or buildout plans to imply returns that the eventual cash flows cannot support. AI can become foundational infrastructure and still leave much of today’s capital impaired.

## The financing matters as much as the spending

The buildout is increasingly financed like heavy industry rather than conventional software. Internal cash flow does not cover every planned data center, so credit markets, including private credit and project finance, are filling the gap. A common structure places buildings, power infrastructure, and chips in a special-purpose vehicle funded by a technology company and outside investors. The technology company then commits to a long-term lease or capacity agreement.

These structures do not make the obligations disappear; they redistribute them and make the chain of exposure harder to see. Debt may sit outside the technology company’s balance sheet, while lease commitments that have not begun receive different accounting treatment from conventional borrowing. Private credit funds can ultimately be backed by pension plans and insurers, creating a path from household retirement savings to data-center residual values through several intermediaries that measure and report risk differently.

The danger is therefore not simply that a public technology stock falls. Private assets reprice slowly, often according to models rather than daily markets, and layered financing agreements can transmit a problem at one facility through cross-defaults or correlated collateral values. Equity-funded overbuilding primarily disappoints shareholders. Debt-funded overbuilding through opaque vehicles can create liquidity problems and distribute losses to institutions whose exposure was not obvious at the start.

## Falling prices make the demand question harder, not easier

AI capability has become dramatically cheaper. Better model architectures, mixture-of-experts systems, quantization, speculative decoding, and specialized inference chips all point toward further reductions in the cost of producing a useful answer. At the same time, reasoning models and agents consume many more tokens per task than chatbots did. A single user request can now trigger planning, tool calls, retries, and verification steps that multiply inference demand.

The optimistic case relies on a Jevons-style effect: as tokens become cheaper, people and software consume so many more of them that total spending continues to rise. That is plausible, but it is an empirical claim rather than a law. Revenue grows only if usage expands faster than unit prices fall. If efficiency improves more quickly than demand, customers receive more capability while infrastructure owners earn less than expected.

This tension is especially important because pricing power looks limited. API inference may already be profitable on a gross-margin basis, while flat-rate subscriptions absorb the losses generated by the heaviest agentic users. The adjustment is therefore more likely to be the withdrawal of unlimited usage and a move toward metering than a broad increase in per-token prices. Compatible APIs, enterprise model-routing systems, and open-weight alternatives make switching easier and impose a durable ceiling on what closed providers can charge.

Open models intensify this pressure. Their importance is not that every open release immediately matches the best closed system on every benchmark. It is that a rapid release cadence steadily commoditizes the large middle of the market. Open weights can serve as customer acquisition for a cloud or infrastructure business even when no one earns much from the weights themselves. If open models also close the gap on coding and agentic work—the tasks with some of the clearest commercial value—the premium available to frontier model providers will narrow further. The economic value may then accrue mainly below the model layer, in compute, and above it, in applications and workflows.

## Profitability has three different meanings

Much of the public debate confuses three separate financial thresholds. First, a provider must earn more from an inference request than it costs to serve that request. Second, the company must cover training, research, product development, sales, and administration. Third, the infrastructure itself must produce a return above its cost of capital after accounting for depreciation and obsolescence.

Evidence that the first threshold has been crossed does not establish the second or third. Gross margin on API usage says little about whether years of frontier training expenses will be recovered, and company-level profitability would not prove that every data center or financing vehicle was a good investment. Traditional software comparisons are also misleading because inference creates a recurring cost for each request rather than a product-development cost that can be amortized across nearly costless copies. Any claim about AI profitability should therefore specify which threshold and which layer of the stack it describes.

## Labor displacement is not a side issue

The economic argument cannot stop at capital expenditure and model margins. The technology is real, it is already replacing some work, and it is likely to replace more. Even where a whole occupation does not disappear, fewer workers may be needed to produce the same output. That can be good for productivity and still be disastrous for the people whose bargaining power and income disappear during the transition.

This is particularly dangerous in the United States because employment is not merely a source of wages. For a large share of working-age Americans, it is also the practical route to affordable health insurance. Stable employment supports mortgage qualification, rent payments, retirement contributions, and access to credit. If displacement becomes large enough that a meaningful share of people cannot find comparable work, the result is not an abstract decline in labor-force participation. It is a population that cannot reliably obtain healthcare, buy homes, build savings, or maintain its existing standard of living.

That feedback loop could turn ugly quickly. Falling employment weakens consumer demand just as the industry needs an enormous expansion in paid AI usage to justify its investment. Housing insecurity and loss of health coverage increase political anger, while the gains from automation may remain concentrated among the owners of models, applications, and infrastructure. New jobs may eventually emerge, as they have in previous technological transitions, but “eventually” is not a social policy. The pace of displacement, the transferability of workers’ skills, and the institutions that support people between jobs matter as much as the long-run number of occupations created.

This leads to a more serious policy question than whether AI should be stopped. It probably cannot be stopped, and suppressing a broadly useful technology would carry its own costs. The question is whether access to healthcare and basic economic security can remain tied so tightly to conventional full-time employment when the technology is explicitly designed to reduce the amount of human labor required. If the gains are privatized while the transition costs are left to displaced workers and local communities, the political response will not be a surprising externality. It will be a predictable consequence of the deployment model.

## What would change the outlook

The central uncertainty is not whether people use AI, but whether that use produces enough durable revenue and measurable output to support the capital committed to it. The most useful signals to watch are:

- **Demand elasticity:** whether total inference spending keeps growing faster than token prices decline.
- **Enterprise returns:** whether adoption produces measurable gains rather than indefinite experimentation budgets.
- **Hardware economics:** whether depreciation schedules begin to reflect faster obsolescence and whether older accelerators retain useful residual value.
- **Financing stress:** whether private-credit marks, refinancing terms, or data-center lease structures begin to reveal losses hidden by slow reporting.
- **Model commoditization:** whether open models close the frontier gap in coding and agentic tasks, weakening premium pricing.
- **Labor-market transmission:** whether AI adoption complements workers, reduces hiring, or produces persistent displacement—and whether displaced workers find comparably paid roles.
- **Physical constraints:** whether power generation, transmission, and interconnection queues continue to bind the buildout more tightly than chips or capital.

Aggregate productivity is the bridge between the technological and financial cases. Investment currently appears clearly as spending; the corresponding economy-wide output is harder to identify. A convincing bull case eventually requires productivity gains large enough to generate customer revenue, support wages and demand, and service the capital stack. If productivity appears only as lower headcount without broader income growth or new demand, the technology may work exactly as promised while undermining the economy expected to pay for it.

## The most plausible synthesis

The strongest bullish and bearish observations can both be true. AI can become indispensable. Token usage can grow enormously. Applications can create real value. At the same time, competition and open weights can compress model margins, hardware can become obsolete faster than expected, and an oversupplied infrastructure layer can fail to earn its cost of capital.

The likely historical pattern is not “AI was fake” or “every investment was justified.” It is that the infrastructure gets built and used, the technology changes the economy, and many of the entities that financed the transition fail to capture the value they helped create. The social outcome is even less automatic. Whether AI ultimately raises living standards will depend not only on model capability, but on who owns the gains, how quickly workers can adapt, and whether healthcare, housing, and economic security remain available to people whose labor the new systems no longer require.
